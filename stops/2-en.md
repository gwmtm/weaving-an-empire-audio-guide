These two mannequins are dressed to resemble the typical elements of clothing worn by
Andean women and men at the time of the Inka.

In order to recreate clothing ensembles from approximately 500 years ago, curators and
conservators used information from multiple sources: textiles excavated from different
archaeological sites, illustrations of Indigenous dress in colonial manuscripts, other art forms
(such as the kero drinking vessels in the display case nearby) and descriptions from a range of
historical documents. Another important source of evidence comes from the sacrificial burials
at mountain summits, where environmental conditions preserved the individuals as they were
originally buried, with their clothing intact on the body. 

The most commonly surviving clothing items are tunics and shoulder mantles. An example of
each can be seen on the wall to the left.

Tunic – 1966.7.217

Men in the Andes during the time of the Inka wore a torso garment known as a tunic. Although
tunics varied in length and width from region to region, they shared some basic structural
similarities. Rather than being tailored from cut and sewn cloth, tunics across the Andes were
woven to fit the wearer and did not require additional modification. The sides were closed with
decorative stitching, and in some cultures short sleeves were added.

Mantle - L0593

Shoulder mantles were an important part of both men’s and women’s dress in many cultures
throughout the Andean region, although their use and composition varied according to gender
and local customs. Regional styles would dictate if it were short or long, the materials used and
patterning choices. An example of a woman’s mantle can be seen on the wall to the left — this
particular example is from an Aymara-speaking community from the highlands.
