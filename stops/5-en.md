With command over the resources needed for the most elaborate forms of textile making, the
Inka developed new clothing types reserved for their highest classes and the emperor.

Expert spinners, weavers and dyers were conscripted to work for the Inka state in producing
the textiles for the most elite people and purposes, intentionally using the costlier materials like
alpaca hair, and the more time-consuming techniques like interlocked tapestry weave.

These two fragments (1930.16.7A and B) are the front and back of the same tunic, called an
“unku” in Quechua, the language used by the Inka. It is woven in the “tokapu waistband” style,
named for the three rows of geometric designs called _tokapu_ which are directly associated with
Inka royalty. Many of the illustrations of Inka rulers in colonial manuscripts show them
wearing _unku_ adorned with _tokapu_ patterns in various arrangements.

The Inka started the trend of having a band in the middle of a man’s tunic – it is not a belt, but a
decorative element woven into the tunic. It is unclear what the individual patterns meant, but
three rows with the _tokapu_ patterning seen in this example in the case was a composition
reserved for men of the royal family. You can also see variations of the “diamond waistband”
tunic – a different type worn by Inka elite -- along the wall in this gallery.

These interlocked tapestry-woven tunics represent the pinnacle of Inka state textile production.
They required large amounts of high-quality materials and the most skilled weavers, likely
requiring more than one weaver to complete.
