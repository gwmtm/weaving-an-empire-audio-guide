While the Inka seat of power was centralized in the capital of Cusco, the vast expanse of the
empire was governed by local officials working on behalf of the empire. While in these roles,
officials were required to dress in garments with Inka design elements to set them apart.

Design elements such as a division into four quadrants, a stepped neckline, and the use of a
red, yellow, green and black color palette were preferred by the Inka and exported to the
regions of the empire. The uniforms of local officials combined these Inka elements with local
preferences for material, iconography and technique.

For example, this tunic (91.282) is a variation on the military key tunic of the Inka elite seen on
the opposite wall, rendered here with a coastal color palette. The composition is organized into
four quadrants like many regional tunics and combines non-Inka imagery of birds and ten-point
stars with one of the Inka’s most elite symbols: a diagonal line and two squares called the “Inka
key.” The Inka key symbol is found in state textiles used in military and ceremonial contexts and
was one of the symbols that could only be used with official permission.

The quadrants are woven in the Inka state colors, but the rest of the tunic features a color
combination of light pink and deep blue which was favored in coastal textiles. The tunic is also
the length of coastal men’s tunics and is the only known example of a tunic with such
prominent Inka influence in these shorter proportions. It likely would have been used by an
official with extremely high status in the local imperial administration.
